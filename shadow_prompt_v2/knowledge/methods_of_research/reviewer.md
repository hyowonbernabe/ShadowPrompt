# Methods of Research — Reviewer

Token-dense compilation of Scientific Communication, Peer Review, and Scientific Journal Article structure. Optimized for fast retrieval by AI agents during examinations.

---

## 1. Scientific Communication

### Two modalities
- **Conference presentations**: tentative form of research output, immediate feedback from peers
- **Publications**: final form, lasting contribution to body of knowledge

### Conferences
- Purpose: networking, collaboration, exchange ideas
- Scope: annual / biennial; international / national / regional
- Function: permanent transparent forum for presenting and critiquing new research
- **Conference proceedings**: collection of papers presented; may be published; most are peer-reviewed and considered legitimate contributions

### Conference presentation types
- **Oral**: 15-30 min (incl Q&A), digital slides, follows academic paper structure (intro, methods, results, conclusion)
- **Poster**: visual summary, displayed in common area, allows personalized interactive discussion

### Academic Journal
- Periodical for a discipline
- Frequency: annual, semi-annual, or quarterly
- Usually peer-reviewed/refereed
- Two submission types: **solicited** (invited) vs **unsolicited** (author submits without being asked)
- Non-peer-reviewed academic/professional publications = **professional magazines**

### Research publication venues
- Research journals
- Conference proceedings published with ISBN or DOI
- Part of a monograph or book

### Refereed Journals
- Dominant form of disseminating research output
- = **valid publication** = **primary literature**
- Properties:
  - Quality: peer-review / refereeing
  - Currency: short / regular interval of publication
  - Readability: short, highly and clearly structured
- **Non-refereed publications = gray literature**

## 2. Peer Review / Refereeing

### Definition
- Mechanism agreed by scientific community to set common standard for members' works
- Process by which research manuscripts are screened prior to publication in journals

### Purpose / Goals
- Filter reliable research outputs
- Prevent dissemination of dubious outputs

### Who reviews
- Experts: formal expertise in disciplinal fields + established credibility as researchers and authors

### Process — Two-step evaluation

**Step 1: Preliminary Assessment (Editor)**
- Is paper appropriate to the journal?
- Has complete elements and sound structure?
  - Required elements present?
  - Tightly organized, logical, compact?
  - Language requires attention?
  - Too long / too brief?
  - Tables/figures necessary/appropriate?
  - References complete and correctly formatted?

**Step 2: Main Assessment (Reviewers/Referees)**
- Scientific merit: significance/contribution to discipline
- Scientific method
- Rating criteria: scientific validity, originality, interest/topical, adequacy/conciseness of summary, appropriateness of methods, correctness of statistical analysis, relevant and logical discussion, adequacy of references
- Recommendations:
  - Accept as is
  - Accept with minor revisions
  - Re-submit with major revisions
  - Reject
- Comments and suggestions included

### Process flow
Author submits → Editorial pre-selection → [Reject] OR [OK → Assign reviewers] → Reviewers review and suggest decision → Editor decides and notifies author → [Accept] / [Reject] / [Revision required → Revise and resubmit → Check revisions → optional re-review] → Camera-ready manuscript → Submit to publisher

### Objectivity mechanisms
- **Double-blind review**: two-way anonymity; both reviewer and author identities concealed from each other; authors must prepare manuscripts to hide identity
- **Single-blind review**: reviewer identity hidden from author; traditional and most common form

### Scale
- 80,000-100,000 journals worldwide

### International standards for top journals
- **ISI-indexed (Web of Science)**: 12,000+ journals
  - Institute for Scientific Information
  - Specializes in citation indexing/analysis
  - **Impact Factor (IF)**: average number of citations to recently published articles in a journal
- **Scopus-indexed**: 16,500 journals
  - "Abstract and citation indexing database of peer-reviewed literature: scientific journals, books and conference proceedings"
  - Owned by Elsevier
- **DOAJ**: Directory of Open Access Journals
- **ERIC**: Education Resources Information Center
- **Scimago**: ranks journals and countries/regions using Scopus data; includes book series, trade journals, conferences, proceedings

### Scopus quality measures (4 numerical metrics per title)
- **h-Index**
- **CiteScore**
- **SJR**: SCImago Journal Rank
- **SNIP**: Source Normalized Impact per Paper

### How to verify Scopus-indexed status
1. Go to www.scopus.com/sources
2. Select Title, Publisher, or ISSN
3. Enter journal name
4. View journal details and database coverage

### Indexing requirements (general)
- Geographic diversity of editors and authors
- Academic contribution to field
- Quality of content
- Regularity of publication

### Importance of these standards
- Reference for scientific community and policy makers
- Professional ranking of individual researchers
- Academic ranking of HEIs (Higher Education Institutions)
- Socio-economic assessment of countries

### Issues with peer review
- Not fool-proof
- Slow process
- Biased against highly innovative outputs
- **Alternative**: post-publication peer review
- Still upheld as crucial for maintaining scientific standard

## 3. Scientific Journal Article — Form and Elements

### Form (question-mapped)
- **Title**: What is the paper all about?
- **Introduction**: What is the problem? Why is it a problem?
- **Material/Methods**: How was the problem solved?
- **Results**: What did the researcher(s) find out?
- **Discussion**: What does it all mean?
- **Conclusion**: wraps up the argument
- **References**: all cited works
- **Acknowledgement** (optional)

### Elements (11 total)
1. Title
2. Abstract
3. Keywords
4. Introduction
5. Method
6. Results / Findings
7. Discussion
8. Conclusion
9. Acknowledgment (optional)
10. Notes (optional)
11. References

---

## 4. Title

- **Most important element**; improperly titled paper may never reach target audience
- Requirements:
  - **Comprehensive**: captures essence; gives thorough idea of content
  - **Brief**: ≤12 substantive words (10-15 recommended by high-impact journals)
  - **Catchy**: attracts interest; contains "hot" words
  - **Informative, specific, concise, understandable**
  - Uses descriptive words associated with paper content
  - Appropriate for intended audience
  - Specific but not so technical only specialists understand
  - Balances technical detail with readability
- **Colon approach**: separate system name / main topic from technical method
- Bad: "The Effects of Social Networking"
- Good: "The Effects of Prolonged Exposure to Social Networking on the Internet on Learning Behavior Patterns Among Teenagers in the Philippines"

---

## 5. Abstract

- **Second most important element**
- Brief summary enabling reader to decide whether to continue reading
- Summative statement answering:
  - What is the paper about? (issue/problem)
  - What does the paper intend to do? (objectives)
  - What are the most important findings? (central argument/significance)
- **Self-contained** (with Title); can be published/read on its own; frequently published alone in indexes
- Prescribed sequence:
  1. **Background statement**: 1-2 sentences
  2. **Objective(s)**: 1 sentence
  3. **Method summary**: brief and to the point
  4. **Results**: specify major points
  5. **Conclusion**: implication to research
- **Length**: 200-300 words; usually single paragraph (~1/3 of a page)
- **Writing strategy**:
  - Write LAST (it summarizes the article)
  - Take whole sentences or key phrases from each section → sequence them → revise for cohesion
  - All info in abstract must also appear in body
  - **Does not require citations**

---

## 6. Keywords

- **Digital breadcrumbs** for other researchers to find the paper
- Used for indexing (together with title) in search databases
- Provide profile of paper for data banks
- May be **author-generated** or **controlled** (chosen from a journal-provided selection)
- Tips:
  - 5-8 keywords (journal-dependent)
  - Key phrases > individual words (e.g., "climate change" vs "climate")
  - Go beyond title words
  - Include acronym AND full term
  - Must appear in body of paper
  - Balance between too broad and too narrow

---

## 7. Introduction

- Comprehensive but concise; 1-2 pages; single continuous essay (no bullets/numbering)
- Addresses 4 areas:
  1. **State of existing knowledge** (literature review: context/background/rationale)
  2. **Issue identified** (problem statement)
  3. **Specifically what study will do** (objectives)
  4. **Why important** (significance/contribution)
- **Movement**: general → specific (inverted triangle)
- **First paragraph**: broad statement → narrows to specific problem
  - Purpose: pique interest, identify what paper addresses, bring reader to edge of knowledge
- **Succeeding paragraphs**: narrow topic; cite other work; vary in length; educate reader on knowledge gap
- **Middle paragraphs**: function as literature review
  - Cite seminal historical contributions
  - Do NOT cite everything — only seminal works that lead directly to the problem
- **Remaining paragraphs**: focus on significant differences between published work and the new contribution

### Literature Review (within Introduction)
- Identifies influential/seminal historical contributions
- Outlines state of knowledge
- Justifies novelty of research contribution
- Usually based on refereed journals
- Gives credit to predecessors; justifies need and novelty

---

## 8. Methodology / Method

- Underlying and unifying aspect of the project
- **Procedural blueprint** of the study
- Specifies the infrastructure behind the solution, not just what was done
- **Must be reproducible**: same methodology applied to same dataset must yield same evaluation results
- Provide diagrams (pipeline, architectural) as needed
- **Results must NOT be included here**
- Research team should track methods/procedures during the study
- For CS papers: dataset lifecycle (acquisition, preprocessing), mathematical formulas, algorithms used, ML model details, metrics/evaluation suite, flowcharts, technology stack

---

## 9. Results / Findings

### Purpose
- Summarizes and presents findings in context of the research question(s)
- Logical sequence, without bias or interpretation
- Use subheadings to organize
- May use text, tables, graphs, illustrations
- Only relevant data tied to the goal
- Presents facts; does NOT analyze or interpret
- Organized in same order as methodology steps

### DOs
- Academic, impartial, objective tone
- Clear topic sentences linking findings to research question
- Establish key findings re research question
- Include **negative findings** (credibility)
- Include **statistical significance tests** if applicable
- Use appropriate presentation method (don't use a table when one sentence works)
- Introduce visuals in the text

### DON'Ts
- Don't discuss/interpret/analyze/speculate (that's Discussion)
- Don't use vague terms or generalizations — be specific
- Don't include every detail (overflow → appendix)
- Don't present raw data that can be summarized/visualized
- Don't present same data in multiple formats
- Don't present data unrelated to research questions or from undocumented procedures
- Don't manipulate data to inflate work

---

## 10. Discussion

- Tells the reader **what the results MEAN** (Results = what you found; Discussion = what it means)
- Shows how results fit broader context (implications, practical applications)
- Movement: **specific → general** (inverse of Introduction)
- Connect back to Introduction in context of research questions/hypotheses
  - Do results support hypotheses?
  - How do results relate to research questions?
  - How consistent with other investigations?
- For **unexpected results**: analyze and discuss why
  - Alternative interpretations?
  - What further research needed?
- **Do NOT introduce new results in Discussion**
- Present **limitations** of research design (delimitations may appear earlier)
- If combined with Results: use sub-headings to distinguish

---

## 11. Tables, Graphs, Images (Sidebar)

### Tables
- Follow format (e.g., APA) or submission entity's spec
- Minimize gridlines, especially for small tables
- For large tables, use gridlines with caution (narrow/gray)
- Label accordingly
- Use captions and legends/titles for readability
- Cite source if reproduced

### Graphs
- Maximize data-ink vs non-data-ink (no unnecessary gridlines, 3D effects, borders, shadows)
- Clear axis labels including units
- Legends inside the plot area

### Images, Screenshots, Diagrams
- Use where graph/table cannot convey the info
- Label at bottom of image
- Crop to relevant area; don't capture entire desktop
- Annotate with arrows, boxes, callouts
- Consistent aspect ratio and border style
- Use drawing tools for diagrams
- Photos for physical observations; scale bars when needed
- **Any non-table visual = Figure**

---

## 12. Conclusion

- Wraps up the paper's argument:
  - Restate objectives
  - Summarize key findings
  - Significance / implications of results
  - Future research directions (limitations from Discussion can recommend future work)
- Verify questions raised in Introduction are answered
- Length: 1-2 paragraphs to 1-2 pages depending on purpose

---

## 13. Acknowledgements

- Acknowledge people who provided significant help (conceptualization, design, conducting research, materials)
- **Optional** section
- **Controversy**: trend in acknowledging reviewers' contributions

---

## 14. References

- All cited works appear in reference list per journal's formatting requirements
- **Preferred sources**: refereed journal articles, research monographs, books
- **Less reliable sources** (avoid as primary): personal communications, unrefereed conference proceedings, website addresses
- Personal communications cited in text are NOT included in Reference list
- URLs must work and lead directly to referenced article

### Referencing mistakes
- **Under-referencing**: paper becomes loosely-founded opinion
- **Over-reliance on few sources**: paper becomes collection/summary
- Achieve balance between originality and adequate referencing

---

## 15. Appendices

- Contain greater-detail information than main body
- Include ONLY appendices referenced in text
- Examples: raw data, maps, extra photographs, sample source code, formula explanations, diagrams of specialized apparatus

---

## 16. Writing Guidelines

### Importance of writing
- Researchers must use language with skill and accuracy → clear, coherent paper
- **Clear thinking precedes clear writing**; writing is itself a productive form of thinking
- Writing helps:
  - Identify what you do and don't know about the topic
  - Clarify and organize thoughts for readers
  - Detect gaps and logical flaws

### Write SOMETHING
- Write often and freely
- **Revision is easier than creation**
- Writers who do 30 min/day are 5-10x more productive
- Final manuscript = flowing narrative (no numbering/bullets in body)
- Writing about a topic enhances understanding of it
- Waiting until thoughts are fully clear = may never start

### Writing to communicate
- Say what you mean — precise word choice, no vague approximations
- Keep primary objective central; everything ties (directly or indirectly) to the research problem
- Provide an overview of what you'll discuss
- Organize from general → specific; use headings/subheadings
- Provide transitional phrases / sentences / paragraphs
- Concrete examples for abstract ideas
- Appropriate punctuation
- Use figures/tables where they present ideas more effectively
- Summarize key ideas at end of each chapter/major section
- **Anticipate multiple drafts** — revise several times whether novice or expert

### Editorial Review (pre-submission)
- Critically self-evaluate before sending to journal
- Guiding principle: revise/edit as if you don't have to do the revisions
- Expect revisions
- Submit the strongest possible article (communication quality + scientific merit)
