## 1. Module 1: Understanding Ethics (slides)

### 1.1 Core definitions

- **Ethics**: the branch of philosophy that studies what is right and wrong, good and bad, in human behavior.
- Key question of ethics: "How should we live? What are our moral obligations and responsibilities?"
- Purpose: gives us frameworks for making reasoned choices and justifying our actions.
- Short version from the slides: ethics is examining our moral beliefs and deciding how we ought to act.
- **Cyber ethics**: using technology, the internet, and digital resources responsibly, fairly, and safely, guided by moral principles. It is ethics applied to the digital world.

### 1.2 Key points about ethics

- Purpose: helps people make fair, responsible, respectful decisions.
- **Ethics is not the same as law.** Laws are rules set by governments. Ethics are moral standards that may go beyond what the law requires. (Common trap: "legal" does not mean "ethical".)
- Shaped by many factors: culture, religion, upbringing, education, personal experience.
- Applied in everyday life, personal and professional.

Why ethics matters: promotes trust, supports justice and fairness, prevents harm and promotes the common good, builds a good reputation.

### 1.3 Branches of ethics

| Branch | What it does | Quick tell |
|---|---|---|
| Normative ethics | Decides what people **should** do | "What ought I do?" |
| Applied ethics | Applies moral rules to real-world issues (business, medical, cyber ethics) | A specific field or issue |
| Meta-ethics | Examines what morality itself **means** | "What does 'good' even mean?" |

Cyber ethics is a form of **applied ethics**.

### 1.4 Key areas of cyber ethics

1. **Privacy**: respecting others' personal info, no collecting, sharing, or misusing data without permission.
2. **Intellectual property**: giving credit, avoiding piracy, respecting copyright.
3. **Cybersecurity responsibility**: strong passwords, no hacking, no spreading malware.
4. **Online behavior**: no cyberbullying, harassment, or spreading false info.
5. **Digital communication etiquette**: respectful and truthful emails, posts, and messages.

Why cyber ethics matters: protects privacy and security, builds trust online, reduces cybercrime, encourages a safe and inclusive online community.

### 1.5 Fundamental problems in identifying ethical standards

1. **Relativism**: what counts as ethical varies across cultures, religions, and individuals, so one universal standard is impossible. Its parts:
   - Cultural differences: acceptable in one society, unacceptable in another.
   - Conflicting values: people rank values differently (truth vs. loyalty).
   - Subjectivity: personal feelings and biases cloud judgment.
   - Changing norms: standards shift over time.
   - Ambiguity in situations: real problems often have no clear right answer, only trade-offs.
2. **Conflicting principles**: different frameworks reach opposite conclusions. Slide example: something serving the common good might violate an individual's rights.
3. **Challenge of application**: abstract theories are hard to apply to messy real situations.
4. **Predicting consequences**: approaches that depend on outcomes (like utilitarian) suffer because outcomes are hard to predict.

### 1.6 The five sources of ethical standards (the five approaches)

This is the most testable part of the course. Expect scenario questions asking "which approach is this?"

| Approach | Core idea | Key question | Slide example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Utilitarian** | Greatest good for the greatest number, least harm. Cost-benefit analysis of **consequences**. | Which option produces the best overall consequences? | Public health policy that saves the most lives even if it inconveniences some. |
| **Rights** | People have fundamental rights that must be respected (truth, privacy, freedom from injury). | Which option best respects the moral rights of everyone involved? | Not sharing someone's personal data without consent, **even if it could benefit many**. |
| **Fairness or Justice** | Treat people fairly and equally based on a defensible standard. "Treat equals equally, and unequals unequally." | Is the distribution of benefits and burdens fair to all? | Same pay for the same work regardless of gender. |
| **Common Good** | Decisions should serve the **community as a whole**. Focus on shared social conditions, institutions, and systems. | What contributes most to the flourishing of the community? | Environmental policies protecting shared air and water. |
| **Virtue** | Focus on the **character of the person**, not the act. Honesty, courage, compassion, integrity. | What would a virtuous person do? | Telling the truth in a hard situation because you are an honest person. |

How to tell them apart fast:

- Counting benefits and harms, "most people", "best outcome" → **Utilitarian**.
- Consent, privacy, dignity, "even if it helps many" → **Rights**.
- Equal treatment, distribution, discrimination, "same for everyone" → **Fairness/Justice**.
- Community, society, shared resources, public systems, institutions → **Common Good**.
- Character, "what kind of person", honesty as a trait, role model → **Virtue**.
- Utilitarian vs. Common Good: utilitarian adds up individual benefits. Common good protects shared conditions that everyone depends on (clean air, trust, institutions).
- Rights vs. Fairness: rights is about what each person is owed as an individual. Fairness is about how benefits and burdens are **distributed** between people.

Module 1 conclusion: there is no one-size-fits-all approach. The five are a toolkit, and the strongest decisions combine several.

## 2. Module 2: Professional Practice in the Computing Profession (slides)

### 2.1 Profession and professional

- **Profession**: a paid occupation that involves prolonged training and a formal qualification. Also, a field of work that has been successfully professionalized.
- **Formal education**: going to a college or university for a degree. Employers prefer it because it is easier to prove.
- **Informal education**: learning a trade from someone else. Also called life experience.
- **Professional**: a member of a profession, or anyone who earns a living from a specific professional activity.
- Being a professional is "a process and a journey". It takes internal drive, delivers judgement, and flows as **Attitude → Behavior → Actions**.
- **Professionalism**: "a way of thinking and living, rather than an accumulation of learning." A profession is more about who you are than what you do.

### 2.2 Fundamental characteristics of a profession (five)

1. **Great responsibility**: deals with matters of vital importance. Carelessness, poor skill, or an ethics breach would seriously damage the client.
2. **Accountability**: professionals hold themselves ultimately accountable for the quality of their work.
3. **Based on specialized, theoretical knowledge**: knowledge beyond outsiders; includes **institutional preparation** (supervised hands-on period under senior members, then compulsory ongoing education).
4. **Ethical constraints**: bound by a profession-specific code of conduct, centered on the client's best interests.
5. **Merit-based**: success comes from merit, not social favor, mandated support, or extortion. Without merit, responsibility, accountability, and ethics become irrelevant.

### 2.3 Lists that look alike (do not mix these up)

| List | Items |
|---|---|
| Four **aspects** of being a professional | Knowing, Doing, Helping, Learning |
| Four **traits** of a profession | Varied activities requiring special skills; Society-centric motivation; Personal standards of excellence; Giving back to society |
| Four **considerations** of professional ethics (relations) | Professional and client; Profession and society; Among professionals; Employee and employer |
| Three **key purposes** of professional ethics | Guidance, Trust, Disciplinary measures |
| Four **common components** of professional ethics | Honesty, Accountability, Confidentiality, Objectivity |

Common components defined:

- **Honesty**: truthful and transparent in all professional dealings.
- **Accountability**: taking responsibility for your actions and decisions.
- **Confidentiality**: respecting the privacy of clients and sensitive info.
- **Objectivity**: deciding on impartial criteria, not personal bias.

Key purposes defined:

- **Guidance**: a framework for working through dilemmas in line with the profession's values.
- **Trust**: following standards builds trust with clients, colleagues, and the public.
- **Disciplinary measures**: violations can lead to penalties or loss of license, which protects the profession's integrity.

### 2.4 What defines a true professional (slide lists)

Behaviors: put customer satisfaction first, make expertise your specialty, do more than expected, do what you say and say what you can do, communicate effectively, follow strong guiding principles, praise peers not yourself, share knowledge, say thank you, keep the right attitude.

Traits: accountable, reliable, respectable, organized, positive attitude, emotional intelligence, professional language, poised, has soft skills, professional appearance, separates personal from professional, manages time well, focused.

### 2.5 Professional responsibilities in IT and CS

Technical: network administration, software development, managing the organization's technology lifecycle (hardware and software maintained, upgraded, replaced). The slides say "these are not enough". You also need:

- Knowledge of national and local laws affecting work (safety, health, environment).
- Principles of management and industrial relations.
- Knowledge about trade unions.
- Responsibility to the community and the country.

### 2.6 Etiquette

- Business social: introduce and welcome people who join, food and alcohol are not the priority, avoid dirty laundry and controversial topics, thank your host. The "24x7 professional".
- Office: greet with a smile, respect privacy, give full attention, be on time, identify yourself on the phone, phones on silent, personal calls in private, "please" and "thank you" in email, fridge rule (if you put it in, you take it out).
- Email: let it simmer (do not send in anger), subject line is your first impression, grammar matters, watch your "voice", avoid ALL CAPS, emoticons, and text-speak, use proper salutations, closings, and signature blocks.

### 2.7 The organization

- **Incorporation**: the legal process that forms a corporation and gives an organization legal existence. Needs **articles of incorporation** and a list of shareholders. Names often carry "Inc." or "Corp."
- **Limited liability**: the business's assets and cash flows are kept separate from the owners' and investors'.
- Incorporation also changes tax treatment (vs. sole proprietorship or partnership) and makes it easier to sell shares and raise capital.

Types of managers:

| Manager | Concern |
|---|---|
| Production | Productivity, efficiency, quality |
| General | The organization as a whole |
| Corporate | Long-term strategy |
| IT | IT activities and services |

Management's main concerns: planning, progress monitoring, getting and allocating resources, quality control.

Functional units:

| Unit | Job |
|---|---|
| Production | Directly creates the products and services sold |
| Quality Management | Keeps quality at the agreed level |
| Sales | Directly sells the product |
| Marketing | Sets the environment the product is sold in, and how the product range develops |
| Finance and Administration | Pays bills, looks after funds |
| Research and Development | How the company can do better what it already does |

Trap: Sales sells. Marketing shapes the environment and the product range.

### 2.8 Structure of the computing profession (two tiers)

- **First level**: the institutions, meaning chartered professional bodies, each covering one or a few related computing disciplines.
- **Second level**: the **computing council**, a chartered umbrella body that recognizes certain institutions as nominated bodies (meaning it is satisfied with their standard of education) and represents the profession as a whole. Examples on the slide: PCS, PELS, PSITE.

Industry affiliations matter because they align you with industry quality standards, bring insight on new IT trends, and keep up continuous professional development.

Building networks in associations: choose the right one, participate actively, connect with members, volunteer for leadership, keep in touch, interact genuinely.

Identifying associations: know your purpose, research, evaluate benefits, try it out, join and engage.

### 2.9 Professional ethics

- **Professional ethics**: your conduct when doing professional work, and the principles and standards that guide a profession's members with internal and external stakeholders.
- Why it matters: workplace decisions are complex, and professional ethics simplifies the decision process.
- An **ethical professional**: admits mistakes and learns from them, respects those who consult them, upholds the profession's reputation, respects authority and the rule of law when managing others, keeps skills current.
- Objectives in higher education: respect and no discrimination, fair treatment, competent teachers, understanding of one's duties, preparation for opportunities and challenges, building moral judgement, recognizing the value of communication, establishing moral standards of behavior.

## 3. Module 3: Frameworks for Making Ethical Decisions (slides)

- Objective: a practical, structured process for identifying and resolving ethical issues.
- Key idea: ethical decisions are about making "the most reasoned and defensible choice", not finding a single right answer.

### 3.1 The five steps

| Step | What you do | Key details |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Recognize an Ethical Issue | Notice a dilemma exists. The first and most crucial step. | Warning signs: right vs. wrong choice, possible harm, conflict of interest, risk of breaking a law or policy. Don't act on impulse, pause. |
| 2. Get the Facts | Gather info so the decision is informed. | Known facts, unknown facts, **stakeholders** (who is affected), have I consulted the right experts? |
| 3. Evaluate Alternative Actions | Brainstorm options and test each against the **five approaches**. | Expect no option to be perfect. |
| 4. Make a Decision and Test It | Pick the best option, then run the **three tests**. | Publicity, Role Model, Universality. |
| 5. Act and Reflect on the Outcome | Carry it out, be ready to explain your reasoning, then review. | Did it match expectations? What did I learn? Would I do it again? |

Slide running example: a coworker takes confidential client data home. Step 1, is it "borrowing" or a privacy violation? Step 2, check if the company allows work-from-home data use. Step 3, options are report, talk to the coworker, or ignore. Step 4, talk privately first. Step 5, coworker apologizes and returns files, private talk avoided escalation.

### 3.2 The three tests (Step 4)

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Publicity** | Would I be comfortable seeing this decision on the front page of a newspaper? |
| **Role Model** | What would my most admired ethical role model do? (Activity version: would I teach a new person to do this?) |
| **Universality** | Would I want this to be a rule everyone must follow? |

Trap: the five **approaches** are used in Step 3 to compare options. The three **tests** are used in Step 4 to check the chosen option.

### 3.3 Why it works and conclusion

- Why it works: **Clarity** (each step builds on the last), **Adaptability** (works for personal, professional, societal dilemmas), **Depth** (encourages growth through reflection).
- Conclusion: a **continuous process** (recognition, evaluation, action, reflection), **a tool, not an answer**, and it strengthens your **moral compass**.

## 4. Applied examples from class activities

### 4.1 Module 3 Overtime Case

Scenario: an IT team leader's team worked overtime for days on a tight deadline. The normal overtime process needs documentation and approval, which the manager skipped. The manager paid the team through a company business expense process instead. No personal gain. HR later said the method was "not ethically appropriate".

Central question: if the work was real and the intent was good, can the method still be unethical? Answer from class: yes.

Main lessons:

- An act has three parts: **intent**, **method**, and **effect**. Good intent covers only one of three. "One out of three is not an ethical act."
- Why it is ethical, not just procedural: the books recorded pay as a business purchase (false record), and employees got cash with no payslip or tax record to prove the income.
- Options: A (keep using the expense route), B (follow the formal overtime process), C (ask HR, Finance, or an authorized manager first), D (document the overtime and request authorized compensation). Class conclusion: C then D.
- Five approaches applied: Utilitarian (same pay is possible without the harm), Rights (employees' right to pay vs. company's right to accurate records, D respects both), Fairness ("would it be fair if every manager did this?"), Common Good (payroll and approvals protect trust for everyone), Virtue (an honest manager escalates openly).
- Tests applied: the true headline is itself damaging (Publicity); nobody would teach this to a new manager (Role Model); if every manager did it, nobody could tell real labor cost or prove income (Universality).
- Step 5 actions from the activity sheet: explain honestly, accept responsibility, show proof the overtime happened, ask for the proper process, correct the transaction, review the policy.
- Competing values listed on the sheet: employee welfare vs. rules, fairness to this team vs. other employees, managerial responsibility vs. compliance, good intention vs. proper process, trust vs. transparency.

### 4.2 Activity 3: AI-powered student surveillance

Question: should the university use AI proctoring even if it invades privacy and may wrongly flag innocent students?

- Utilitarian: protects honest students, but a false flag hurts an innocent one. Worth it only if errors are rare and caught before any penalty.
- Rights: a webcam and mic in a bedroom is a privacy invasion, worse with no appeal.
- Fairness: a disability, a tic, or a noisy home can get someone flagged.
- Common Good: fair exams protect the value of grades, but surveillance nobody trusts breeds resentment.
- Virtue: a responsible school does not let a machine hand down a verdict without a human check.
- Position: yes, with human review before penalties, deletion after grading, an alternative for students without private space, and clear disclosure.

### 5.2 Stuxnet (discovered 2010)

- What happened: a highly advanced worm targeted Siemens industrial controllers (PLCs, the small computers that run machinery) at Iran's Natanz uranium enrichment site. It changed centrifuge speeds to wreck them while showing normal readings to operators. It spread by USB drives and used four zero-day flaws (a **zero-day** is a flaw the vendor does not know about yet, so no patch exists).
- Why it matters: the first known cyberweapon to cause **physical** damage. Widely reported as a US and Israeli operation ("Olympic Games").
- Ethical issues: state-sponsored cyberwarfare and whether it can be justified (preventing nuclear weapons vs. attacking another state); collateral damage, since it escaped and infected computers worldwide; governments stockpiling zero-days instead of reporting them; lack of accountability.
- Lessons: air-gapped systems (cut off from the internet) can still be infected through USB; critical infrastructure needs strong OT security; cyberweapons can be copied and reused by others.

### 5.3 Sony Pictures hack (November 2014)

- What happened: a group calling itself the "Guardians of Peace" broke into Sony Pictures, wiped computers, and leaked unreleased films, executives' emails, scripts, and employee data (salaries, Social Security numbers, health info). The attack was tied to *The Interview*, a comedy about killing Kim Jong-un. The FBI blamed North Korea. After threats against theaters, Sony pulled the wide theatrical release, then released the film online and in limited theaters.
- Ethical issues: employees' privacy exposed through no fault of their own; whether media should publish stolen emails; free expression vs. giving in to threats; weak security practices (reports of passwords kept in files named "passwords").
- Lessons: protect employee data as seriously as business data; plan incident response; leaked emails show why professional email etiquette matters.

### 5.4 Cambridge Analytica (harvested 2014, exposed March 2018)

- What happened: researcher Aleksandr Kogan's quiz app "thisisyourdigitallife" was used by about 270,000 people, but Facebook's rules then let the app collect their **friends'** data too, reaching up to 87 million profiles. Cambridge Analytica, a political consulting firm, used the data to build psychological profiles for targeted political ads (linked to the 2016 US election). Whistleblower Christopher Wylie exposed it through The Guardian/Observer and the New York Times.
- Aftermath: Mark Zuckerberg testified before the US Congress (April 2018). Cambridge Analytica shut down (May 2018). The US FTC fined Facebook $5 billion (2019). The Philippines had about 1.18 million affected users, the most outside the US, and the National Privacy Commission investigated.
- Ethical issues: **informed consent** (friends never agreed); **secondary use** of data for a purpose other than the one collected for; manipulation of voters and threat to democracy; platform responsibility.
- Lessons: it was a data **misuse** scandal, not a hack. Consent must be specific. Data shared for one purpose should not be reused for another. Fits the Rights approach and the Data Privacy Act principles.

### 5.5 WannaCry (May 12, 2017)

- What happened: ransomware (malware that locks files and demands payment) spread on its own like a worm to over 200,000 computers in about 150 countries. It demanded $300 to $600 in bitcoin. It used **EternalBlue**, an exploit for a Windows SMB flaw developed by the US NSA and leaked by the "Shadow Brokers" group. Microsoft had already released a patch (MS17-010) in March 2017. The UK's National Health Service was badly hit, with cancelled appointments and diverted ambulances.
- Stopped by: security researcher Marcus Hutchins, who registered a "kill switch" domain written into the malware.
- Attribution: North Korea's Lazarus Group (US and UK, December 2017).
- Ethical issues: governments hoarding vulnerabilities instead of telling vendors; organizations failing to patch or still running unsupported Windows XP; attacks on hospitals putting lives at risk; whether to pay ransom.
- Lessons: patch promptly; keep backups; replace unsupported systems; disclose vulnerabilities responsibly.

### 5.6 Equifax breach (2017)

- What happened: attackers used a known flaw in Apache Struts (a web framework) that had a patch available since March 2017, which Equifax had not applied. From May to July 2017 they took data on about 147 million people: names, Social Security numbers, birth dates, addresses, some driver's license numbers, and about 209,000 credit card numbers. An expired security certificate on a monitoring tool meant the stolen traffic went unnoticed for months. Equifax found it on July 29 and announced it on September 7, 2017.
- Aftermath: executives sold stock before the public announcement (one was convicted of insider trading); a settlement of up to about $700 million with the FTC, CFPB, and US states (2019); the CEO resigned; four Chinese military hackers were indicted in 2020.
- Ethical issues: failure of **due care** (not patching a known flaw); delayed disclosure; insider trading; people's data held by a company they never chose to deal with.
- Lessons: patch management is an ethical duty, not only a technical one; disclose quickly and honestly; accountability for data custodians. Good example of a vulnerability that a risk assessment should have flagged.

### 5.7 SolarWinds (discovered December 2020)

- What happened: attackers broke into SolarWinds' build system and slipped a backdoor (called SUNBURST) into updates for its Orion network monitoring software in 2020. About 18,000 customers installed the poisoned update. The attackers then went deeper into selected targets, including several US federal agencies and tech firms. Security firm FireEye found it after being breached itself. Attributed to Russia's foreign intelligence service (SVR, APT29).
- Key term: **supply chain attack**, attacking a trusted supplier so its own customers install the malware.
- Ethical issues: vendor responsibility for secure software; trust in signed updates; reports of a weak password ("solarwinds123") on an update server; honesty to investors (the US SEC charged SolarWinds and its security chief in 2023 over misleading security statements); state espionage.
- Lessons: you inherit your vendors' risk (third-party risk); verify software builds; zero trust (trust nothing by default, verify everything).

### 5.8 Colonial Pipeline (May 7, 2021)

- What happened: the DarkSide ransomware group got in through an old VPN account whose password had leaked, and the account had no multi-factor authentication. The ransomware hit Colonial's business IT systems. Colonial shut down its 5,500-mile pipeline, which carries about 45% of the US East Coast's fuel, for about six days as a precaution. Panic buying and fuel shortages followed.
- Ransom: Colonial paid about 75 bitcoin (about $4.4 million). The US Justice Department later recovered about $2.3 million of it.
- Ethical issues: whether paying ransom is right (restores service fast but funds criminals and invites more attacks); duty of critical infrastructure operators to the public; basic security failures (no MFA, unused account left active); transparency with government and the public.
- Lessons: use MFA; remove unused accounts; separate IT and OT networks; have an incident response plan. Utilitarian reasoning was likely used to justify paying.

### 5.9 Mapping cases to approaches (handy for scenario MCQs)

- Paying ransom to restore fuel for millions (Colonial) → Utilitarian reasoning.
- Using friends' data without consent (Cambridge Analytica) → Rights violation.
- Hospitals hit while patches existed (WannaCry) → Common Good (shared public systems).
- Executives selling stock before disclosure (Equifax) → Virtue and Fairness failure (insiders gained over the public).
- State-built cyberweapon that escaped (Stuxnet) → conflicting principles, national security vs. global harm.

## 6. Information Security Risk Management (general knowledge, not from posted slides)

### 6.1 Definitions

- **ISRM**: the ongoing process of finding, assessing, and treating risks to an organization's information and systems, so the risk stays at a level the organization accepts.
- **Asset**: anything of value to the organization (data, hardware, software, people, reputation).
- **Threat**: anything that could cause harm by exploiting a weakness (hacker, malware, flood, careless employee).
- **Threat agent or actor**: the person or thing that carries out the threat.
- **Vulnerability**: a weakness that a threat can exploit (unpatched software, weak password, no MFA).
- **Exploit**: the actual method or tool used to take advantage of a vulnerability.
- **Risk**: the chance that a threat exploits a vulnerability and causes harm to an asset.
- **Impact**: how much damage results if it happens.
- **Likelihood**: how probable it is to happen.
- **Control (safeguard, countermeasure)**: something that reduces risk (firewall, policy, training, lock).
- **Exposure**: being open to possible loss from a threat.

Formulas:

- Risk = Likelihood x Impact
- Risk = Threat x Vulnerability x Impact (or x Asset Value)
- If any factor is zero, risk is zero. No vulnerability, no risk from that threat.

Trap: a **threat** is the danger (the attacker). A **vulnerability** is the weakness (the open door). A **risk** is the chance of loss when both meet.

### 6.2 CIA triad

| Goal | Meaning | Attack against it |
|---|---|---|
| **Confidentiality** | Only authorized people can see the data | Data breach, eavesdropping (Equifax, Cambridge Analytica) |
| **Integrity** | Data stays accurate and unaltered | Tampering (Stuxnet faking readings, SolarWinds altered updates) |
| **Availability** | Systems and data are there when needed | Ransomware, DDoS (WannaCry, Colonial Pipeline) |

Related terms: **authentication** (proving who you are), **authorization** (what you are allowed to do), **non-repudiation** (you cannot deny you did something, e.g. digital signatures).

### 6.3 Risk management process (general steps)

1. **Identify assets** and their value.
2. **Identify threats**.
3. **Identify vulnerabilities**.
4. **Analyze risk**: estimate likelihood and impact.
5. **Evaluate risk**: rank risks and compare against risk appetite.
6. **Treat risk**: choose avoid, mitigate, transfer, or accept.
7. **Monitor and review**: risks change, so repeat.
8. Communicate and document throughout (often in a **risk register**, a list of risks with owner, rating, and treatment).

Risk assessment = identification + analysis + evaluation (steps 2 to 5). Risk management = the whole cycle including treatment and monitoring.

### 6.4 Qualitative vs. quantitative risk analysis

| | Qualitative | Quantitative |
|---|---|---|
| Uses | Ratings like High, Medium, Low; risk matrix (heat map) | Numbers, money values |
| Pros | Fast, simple, works without hard data | Precise, supports cost-benefit decisions |
| Cons | Subjective | Needs good data, time-consuming |

Quantitative formulas:

- **AV (Asset Value)**: what the asset is worth.
- **EF (Exposure Factor)**: the percentage of the asset lost in one incident.
- **SLE (Single Loss Expectancy)** = AV x EF. Loss from one incident.
- **ARO (Annualized Rate of Occurrence)**: how many times per year it is expected. Once every 4 years means ARO = 0.25.
- **ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy)** = SLE x ARO. Expected loss per year.

Worked example: a server worth ₱1,000,000 (AV). A flood would destroy 40% of it (EF), so SLE = ₱400,000. A flood happens once every 10 years (ARO = 0.1), so ALE = ₱40,000.

Cost-benefit rule: a control is worth it if (ALE before − ALE after − yearly cost of the control) is positive. Do not spend more per year protecting an asset than the yearly loss you avoid.

### 6.5 Risk treatment options

| Option | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Avoid** | Stop the risky activity entirely | Do not store credit card numbers at all |
| **Mitigate (reduce)** | Add controls to lower likelihood or impact | Patch, MFA, firewall, backups |
| **Transfer (share)** | Shift the financial impact to another party | Cyber insurance, outsourcing to a provider |
| **Accept (retain)** | Knowingly live with it, usually because it is small or fixing costs more | Accept the risk of a minor website outage |

Traps:

- Transfer moves the **financial** loss, not the responsibility. The organization is still accountable for the breach (and under the Data Privacy Act the controller stays liable).
- Accepting is a valid choice only if it is a conscious, documented decision. Ignoring a risk is not acceptance.
- Avoid ≠ mitigate. Avoiding removes the activity. Mitigating keeps the activity and adds controls.

### 6.6 More key terms

- **Inherent risk**: risk before any controls are applied.
- **Residual risk**: risk left over after controls. Never zero. Residual risk is what management accepts.
- **Risk appetite**: how much risk the organization is willing to take overall to reach its goals (big-picture).
- **Risk tolerance**: the acceptable variation around that appetite for a specific risk (narrower, measurable).
- **Risk owner**: the person accountable for managing a given risk.
- **Due care**: doing what a reasonable person would do to protect assets (e.g. patching). **Due diligence**: investigating and understanding risks before acting. (Equifax failed due care.)

Control types by function:

- **Preventive**: stops an incident (firewall, locks, MFA).
- **Detective**: finds an incident (IDS, audit logs, CCTV).
- **Corrective**: fixes after an incident (backups, restore, patching after attack).
- Also deterrent (warning signs), recovery (disaster recovery plan), compensating (substitute control).

Control types by nature: **administrative** (policies, training), **technical or logical** (encryption, firewalls), **physical** (guards, locks).

### 6.7 Frameworks and standards

- **ISO/IEC 27001**: the international standard for an Information Security Management System (ISMS). Organizations get certified against it.
- **ISO/IEC 27002**: the catalog of security controls that supports 27001.
- **ISO/IEC 27005**: guidance specifically for **information security risk management**. Process: context establishment, risk assessment (identification, analysis, evaluation), risk treatment, risk acceptance, plus ongoing communication and monitoring.
- **ISO 31000**: general risk management for any kind of risk, not only IT.
- **NIST SP 800-30**: US guide for conducting **risk assessments**. Steps: prepare, conduct, communicate, maintain.
- **NIST RMF (SP 800-37)**: Risk Management Framework, seven steps: Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize, Monitor.
- **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF 2.0)**: six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover.
- Others you may see: COBIT (IT governance), OCTAVE (risk assessment method from Carnegie Mellon), FAIR (quantitative risk method).

Trap: 27001 = the ISMS you certify. 27005 = the risk management guide. SP 800-30 = risk assessment. SP 800-37 = RMF.

### 6.8 Why ISRM is an ethics topic

- Professionals owe clients **due care** (Module 2's "great responsibility" and "accountability").
- Underinvesting in security shifts risk onto customers who never agreed to it (Equifax).
- Choosing to accept or transfer a risk is a value judgment, which can be tested with the five approaches.

## 7. Philippine laws (general knowledge, not from posted slides)

### 7.1 RA 10173, Data Privacy Act of 2012

- Protects personal information in information and communications systems, public and private.
- Created the **National Privacy Commission (NPC)**, which enforces the law.
- Three principles: **transparency**, **legitimate purpose**, **proportionality**.
- **Personal Information Controller (PIC)**: decides what data is collected and why. **Personal Information Processor (PIP)**: processes data on the controller's behalf.
- **Sensitive personal information** includes race, ethnicity, religion, health, government IDs, and similar. Needs stricter handling.
- Data subject rights: to be informed, to access, to object, to erasure or blocking, to rectification, to data portability, to damages, and to file a complaint.
- Breach notification: notify the NPC and affected people within **72 hours** of learning of a serious breach (under NPC rules).
- Applies to Cambridge Analytica (NPC investigated Facebook) and any company handling Filipino data.

### 7.2 RA 10175, Cybercrime Prevention Act of 2012

- Defines and punishes cybercrimes. Three groups:
  1. Offenses against the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of data and systems: illegal access, illegal interception, data interference, system interference, misuse of devices, cybersquatting.
  2. Computer-related offenses: computer-related forgery, fraud, and identity theft.
  3. Content-related offenses: cybersex, child pornography, unsolicited commercial communications (spam), and **cyber libel**.
- Crimes in the Revised Penal Code done through ICT get a penalty **one degree higher**.
- *Disini v. Secretary of Justice* (2014): the Supreme Court upheld most of the law, including online libel for the original author, but struck down the spam provision, real-time traffic data collection without a warrant, and the DOJ's power to block access without a court order.
- Created the Cybercrime Investigation and Coordinating Center (CICC) and the DOJ Office of Cybercrime.

### 7.3 RA 8792, Electronic Commerce Act of 2000

- Gives electronic documents and electronic signatures the same legal recognition as paper ones.
- First Philippine law to penalize **hacking** and **piracy** through computers.
- Passed shortly after the **ILOVEYOU virus** (2000), created by Filipino student Onel de Guzman, who could not be charged because no law covered it then.

### 7.4 Which law fits?

| Scenario | Law |
|---|---|
| Company leaks customer data or collects it without consent | RA 10173 (Data Privacy Act) |
| Someone hacks a system, posts libel online, or steals an identity online | RA 10175 (Cybercrime Prevention Act) |
| Is an e-signature valid? Early hacking case | RA 8792 (E-Commerce Act) |
| Software piracy, copyright | RA 8293 (Intellectual Property Code) |

## 8. Common traps and distinctions

- Legal ≠ ethical. Ethics can demand more than the law.
- Normative (what should we do) vs. applied (specific field) vs. meta-ethics (what morality means).
- "Treat equals equally, unequals unequally" is **Fairness/Justice**, not Rights.
- "Greatest good for the greatest number" is **Utilitarian**, not Common Good.
- "What would a good person do?" is **Virtue**. The **Role Model Test** sounds similar but belongs to Step 4.
- Virtue focuses on the **agent's character**. The other four focus on the action or its effects.
- Utilitarian's weakness: predicting consequences. Rights' strength: protects individuals from being sacrificed for the majority.
- Step 1 is "the first and most crucial step". Stakeholders are identified in Step 2. The five approaches are used in Step 3. The three tests are in Step 4. Reflection is Step 5.
- Four aspects (Knowing, Doing, Helping, Learning) vs. four traits (special skills, society-centric, personal excellence, giving back).
- Components of professional ethics: Honesty, Accountability, Confidentiality, **Objectivity** (not "loyalty" or "profit").
- Limited liability comes from incorporation.
- Computing council = second tier, umbrella body. Institutions = first tier.
- Threat vs. vulnerability vs. risk. Inherent vs. residual. Appetite vs. tolerance.
- SLE = AV x EF. ALE = SLE x ARO. Do not multiply AV by ARO directly.
- Insurance = transfer. Patching = mitigate. Dropping the service = avoid. Doing nothing on purpose = accept.
- Cambridge Analytica was misuse, not a hack. SolarWinds was a supply chain attack. WannaCry and Colonial were ransomware. Stuxnet was sabotage of physical equipment.
