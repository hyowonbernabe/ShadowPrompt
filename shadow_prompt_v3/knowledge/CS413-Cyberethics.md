## 1. The Ethical Decision-Making Framework (core theory)

### 1.1 Purpose and framing

- Objective (as stated in the slides): "To provide a practical, structured process for identifying and resolving ethical issues."
- Method: break down a complex ethical problem into a series of manageable steps.
- Key idea: "Ethical decisions are not about finding a single 'right' answer, but about making the most reasoned and defensible choice."
- The framework is a five-step process. It is described at the end as "A Tool, Not an Answer," meaning it guides thinking rather than guaranteeing a correct outcome, and as a continuous cycle (recognition, evaluation, action, reflection), not a one-time event.

### 1.2 Step 1: Recognize an Ethical Issue

The first and most crucial step is to identify that an ethical dilemma exists.

Warning signs (questions to ask):
- Does a decision involve a choice between right and wrong?
- Could this action cause harm to a person or group?
- Is there a potential conflict of interest?
- Is there a risk of violating a law, rule, or policy?

Initial action: don't act impulsively. Pause and acknowledge the ethical nature of the situation.

Purpose (from the summary table): identify when values, principles, or rights are at stake. Key questions: is this a moral question or just a legal/technical one? Could it harm someone or the community?

Example given in the slides: you see a coworker taking confidential client data home. Is it just "borrowing," or a privacy violation?

### 1.3 Step 2: Get the Facts

Gather all relevant information to avoid making an uninformed decision.

Key questions:
- What are the known facts of the situation?
- What are the unknown or uncertain facts?
- Who are the stakeholders? Who will be affected by this decision?
- Have I consulted all relevant experts or people with important information?

Goal: a clear and objective understanding of the situation before moving forward.

Example given: before confronting the coworker over the data, you find out whether the company allows work-from-home data use.

### 1.4 Step 3: Evaluate Alternative Actions

Brainstorm a range of possible actions and evaluate each one using different ethical frameworks. This step is where the Five Ethical Approaches (see Section 2) get applied as tools.

Output: you will likely find that no single option is perfect, but the evaluation clarifies the strengths and weaknesses of each choice.

Example given: possible actions are report to manager, talk to coworker, or ignore it. You weigh the pros and cons of each.

### 1.5 Step 4: Make a Decision and Test It

Choose the best alternative based on the Step 3 evaluation, then "test" it before acting. Three tests:

- **The Publicity Test**: Would you feel comfortable with your decision being publicized on the front page of a newspaper?
- **The Role Model Test**: What would your most admired ethical role model do in this situation?
- **The Universality Test**: Would you want this to be a universal rule that everyone must follow?

Final action: if the decision passes these tests, you are ready to move on.

Example given: you decide to talk privately with the coworker first, believing it's fair and respectful.

### 1.6 Step 5: Act and Reflect on the Outcome

Put the decision into action, then take time to review the results.

During the action: be prepared to explain your reasoning to others.

After the action, ask:
- Did the outcome align with your expectations?
- What did you learn from this experience?
- Would you make the same decision again?

Purpose: this reflection step is crucial for continuous moral growth and for improving future decision-making.

Example given: the coworker apologizes and returns the files. You reflect that private dialogue avoided escalation.

### 1.8 Why the framework works (lecture's own justification)

- **Clarity**: each step builds logically, from spotting the problem to learning from it.
- **Adaptability**: the model works across ethical dilemmas, personal, professional, or societal.
- **Depth**: it doesn't just guide decisions, it encourages ethical growth through reflection and insight.

### 1.9 Conclusion points from the lecture

- A continuous process: ethical decision-making is not a one-time event, but a continuous cycle of recognition, evaluation, action, and reflection.
- A tool, not an answer: the framework guides thinking, it is not a rigid formula that guarantees the "right" answer.
- Your moral compass: using the framework strengthens ethical judgment and helps navigate complex challenges with confidence.

## 2. The Five Ethical Approaches (definitions, exact wording preserved)

These are the analytical lenses applied in Step 3 of the framework. Definitions below combine the exact phrasing from the core lecture slide ("Step 3: Evaluate Alternative Actions") and the wording used in the Module 3 case activity sheet's evaluation table, since the activity sheet phrases the questions slightly differently and both phrasings could appear on an exam.

### 2.1 Utilitarian approach

- Lecture phrasing: "Which option produces the most good and the least harm?"
- Case activity sheet phrasing: "Which option produces the greatest overall benefit and least harm for the employees and organization?"
- Core concept: judges an action by its consequences, weighing total benefit against total harm across everyone affected.

### 2.2 Rights approach

- Lecture phrasing: "Which option best respects the rights of all involved?"
- Case activity sheet phrasing: "Do employees have a right to fair compensation for legitimate work? Does the organization have a right to accurate and proper use of its resources?"
- Core concept: judges an action by whether it respects the moral rights (such as autonomy, privacy, truthful treatment) of everyone involved, not just one party.

### 2.3 Fairness/Justice approach

- Lecture phrasing: "Which option treats people equally or proportionally?"
- Case activity sheet phrasing: "Would it be fair if every manager used the same method whenever employees worked overtime?"
- Core concept: judges an action by whether it treats similarly situated people consistently, and whether the benefit or burden it creates could be distributed evenly without becoming a special privilege for some.

### 2.4 Common Good approach

- Lecture phrasing: "Which option serves the community and its shared values?"
- Case activity sheet phrasing: "Which decision protects trust, accountability, and the organization's systems for everyone?"
- Core concept: judges an action by whether it protects the shared systems, institutions, and trust that everyone in the community depends on, not just the immediate parties to the decision.

### 2.5 Virtue approach

- Lecture phrasing: "Which option aligns with the virtues you value most (e.g., honesty, integrity)?"
- Case activity sheet phrasing: "What would an honest, responsible, fair, and trustworthy manager do?"
- Core concept: judges an action by what a person of good character (honest, responsible, fair, trustworthy) would do, focusing on the character and habits behind the choice rather than only its outcome or its rule-following.

### 2.6 Using the Five Approaches together

The framework does not treat the five approaches as competing verdicts to average. Step 3 of the lecture says to evaluate each alternative action against each approach and expect that "no single option is perfect." The value of the exercise is in clarifying strengths and weaknesses of each option, not in producing a single numeric score.

The Module 3 case activity sheet adds a **Challenge Question** that is likely testable: "What if one ethical approach supports the outcome, but another raises concerns about the method used? How should the manager respond?" This directly tests the distinction between judging an action by its outcome (utilitarian-style) versus judging it by the method or process used to reach that outcome (rights, fairness, virtue-style). The debate materials (Section 4 below) work through exactly this tension using the overtime case.

## 3. The Three Decision Tests (Step 4 detail)

These three tests are used to check a decision before acting on it, and are commonly tested individually since each has a distinct logic.

1. **Publicity Test**: also called the "front page" test. Ask whether you would be comfortable if this decision and the reasoning behind it were made public (front page of a newspaper, or in the case activity sheet's phrasing, "front page of a newspaper or social media"). The logic: if the honest, accurate description of what you did is the damaging part, the problem is the act itself, not how it might be spun.
2. **Role Model Test**: ask what your most admired ethical role model would do, or (per the case activity sheet) whether you would teach a new person to follow the same approach. The logic: if you would not teach it to someone else as the right way to act, that is a signal you already know it is wrong.
3. **Universality Test**: also framed as Kant's-style universalizability. Ask whether you would want this to be a universal rule that everyone must follow, or what would happen if everyone in the same position did the same thing. The logic: a "rule" that only works because almost nobody follows it (it depends on staying rare or hidden) is not really a sound rule, it is a loophole that collapses under general use.

## 4. Applying the Frameworks: The Overtime Case (Module 3 case debate)

This is a class case-analysis and moot-style debate exercise applying the five-step framework and the five ethical approaches to a specific workplace scenario. It is a class exercise, not a real event, but it is the most fully worked example of the framework in practice.

### 4.1 The case scenario (as given in the activity brief)

A team leader/manager in an IT department has a team that rendered overtime for multiple days to meet a tight project deadline. The manager believes the team deserves compensation. However, the company's normal process for recording and charging overtime requires specific documentation and approval, which the manager did not obtain. Instead, the manager used an official company business-related expense/process (an expense account, normally used for the company buying things for work) to account for the team's overtime pay. The manager's intention was not personal benefit, but to protect and compensate the team.

Later, HR reviews the transaction and says that the way the expense/process was used was "not ethically appropriate," even though the team genuinely worked the overtime and the manager's intent was to help them.

**Central ethical question posed by the activity**: "If the work was genuinely performed and the manager's intention was to help the employees, can the way the manager handled the compensation still be unethical?"

**Framing question repeated in the debate materials**: "Can you do the right thing for the right reason, but still do it the wrong way?"

The activity brief's own answer key concept: "In professional IT practice, ethical decision-making is not only about good intentions. It also considers authorization, transparency, fairness, rights, professional responsibility, organizational policy, and the consequences of allowing the same action to become a general practice."

### 4.2 Why this counts as an ethical issue, not just a procedural one

Key distinction argued in the HR brief: some rules are pure housekeeping (example given: parking in your assigned slot), and breaking them hurts nobody. Ethics applies when what was done reaches and affects a person, so the relevant question is not "was a rule broken" but "was something done to somebody."

Two things push this case past mere paperwork, per the HR brief:
1. The company's records no longer match what actually happened: overtime pay was recorded as a business purchase, which it was not.
2. The team ended up worse off in a concrete way: payroll pay generates a payslip and a tax record (used for things like loan applications or job applications), while expense-account pay leaves no such record. The employees ended up with cash they could not prove they earned, and no record they worked those hours.

This is why HR characterized it as "not ethically appropriate" rather than "against policy": calling it unethical means it would still be wrong even in a company with no formal rules at all, because a record that doesn't match reality is inherently dishonest, and because no individual should move company money unilaterally just by being confident they are right.

### 4.3 Step 1 applied: naming the issue

The manager solved a real problem (unpaid, deserved overtime) using a method that (a) recorded the payment as something it was not, and (b) released pay without proper authority. This is characterized as "real problem, dishonest fix."

Stakeholders identified: the team (who received money with no formal record), the company (whose financial books no longer reflect reality), and future employees under other managers (since if this method is seen to work, it could spread).

**Key theoretical point on intentions**: An action is judged on three dimensions: (1) what you were trying to do (intent), (2) how you did it (method), and (3) what happened as a result (effect/outcome). Good intentions only cover the first of these three. Intent affects how much blame a person carries, but it does not, by itself, determine whether the act was honest or ethical. This "one out of three" framing (intent passes, method fails, effect fails) is used repeatedly throughout the debate script as the central analytical structure.

### 4.4 Step 2 applied: facts versus assumptions

Known facts (both from the case brief and the official activity sheet's fact table):
- Employees actually worked overtime.
- The manager wanted to recognize/compensate the employees, not personally benefit.
- An official company expense process/mechanism was used to account for the overtime.
- HR questioned the transaction.
- HR determined the manner of handling the transaction was not ethically appropriate.
- The team's work contributed to the project.

Unknown / needs verification (per the official activity sheet):
- Was the overtime formally approved?
- What exactly does the official overtime policy require?
- Was the manager authorized to use the expense process this way?
- Were other employees/managers treated similarly?
- Was the transaction accurately documented?
- Did anyone receive a personal financial benefit?

**Reasoning technique demonstrated (the "fork")**: when a fact is genuinely unknown and unverifiable from the case materials, argue it both ways and show your conclusion holds regardless. Example used in the debate script: Was the overtime pre-approved? If yes, there was no reason to bypass the normal payroll route. If no, unapproved work was paid without sign-off. Either way, the argument for using the correct channel from the start still holds. The debate script explicitly warns against claiming facts the source material does not provide, since the opposing side in a debate could contradict an invented fact.

**Two conclusions reached by plain reasoning, without inventing facts**:
1. Overtime pay is not a business expense category, so routing it through the expense process necessarily means the company's books describe that money as something it was not.
2. Money paid through the expense route did not go through payroll, meaning there is no payroll entry, no tax withheld, and no record on a tax certificate for those hours.

### 4.5 Step 3 applied: the four options and the five approaches

The four alternative actions defined by the official activity sheet:
- **Option A**: Continue using the business-related expense mechanism to compensate the team.
- **Option B**: Follow the formal overtime process, including required documentation and approval.
- **Option C**: Ask HR, Finance, or an authorized manager for guidance before taking action.
- **Option D**: Do not use the expense mechanism; formally document the overtime and request an authorized form of compensation or recognition.

The HR-side debate script evaluates these four options against each of the five ethical approaches. This is the fullest practical illustration of "using the frameworks" available in the source materials:

- **Utilitarian**: Option A (expense route) only appears to maximize good if you stop counting on the day the money is paid. Counting further: the team ends up holding unprovable income, the books misstate company spending, and the broken/slow approval process that caused the workaround remains unfixed for the next team. Options C (ask first) and D (document and request) deliver the same pay to the same people without those harms. Conclusion argued: when a good outcome is achievable without the harm, the harm is not justified by the outcome.
- **Rights**: Two rights are in tension: employees' right to be paid for hours worked, and the organization's right to records that accurately describe its own transactions. Option A serves the first right by violating the second, and arguably serves the first poorly too, since unrecorded pay cannot later be proven as income. Option D is argued to be the only option that honors both rights simultaneously.
- **Fairness/Justice**: Framed as the case's own fairness test: would it be fair if every manager used the expense account whenever their team worked overtime? Argued answer: what the manager actually distributed was not overtime pay per se, but the advantage of having a boss willing to misuse the expense account, which cannot be distributed evenly across a company (it depends entirely on who your manager happens to be).
- **Common Good**: Payroll, approval processes, and financial books exist so that people not present for a transaction can still trust that it reflects reality. The expense-route workaround (Option A) only "works" while it remains rare and unexamined. If normalized, there would be no way to distinguish a well-intentioned workaround from actual theft, since both would look identical on the books. Escalating through proper channels (Option C) is argued to protect this shared system for everyone, not just the one team.
- **Virtue**: An honest manager does not route money in a way that disguises its true purpose in the records, and does not unilaterally decide that approval requirements do not apply to their situation. A virtuous response is to escalate openly (put their name on a request) and accept the risk of being told no. The debate script frames this as costing the manager something, which is precisely what makes it a virtue rather than a convenience.

**Challenge Question response (method versus outcome)**: The debate script's answer to "what if one approach likes the outcome but another objects to the method" is that liking an outcome does not retroactively justify every method used to reach it, especially when an honest method (asking first, documenting properly) was available and would have produced the same outcome.

Notably, the debate script observes that across all five approaches, the same practical conclusion recurs: avoid the expense-route workaround (Option A) and instead escalate/document properly (Options C and D). This convergence across independently-reasoned approaches is used as a rhetorical strength in the debate.

### 4.6 Step 4 applied: decision and the three tests

The HR-side decision reached: pursue Option C (ask first) followed by Option D (document and request properly). Justification given: same money reaches the same people, nothing false enters the records, and it costs only one additional conversation; the only real risk is being told no, which does not justify falsifying records instead.

Applying the three tests to the manager's actual choice (using the expense route):
- **Publicity Test**: The accurate, honest headline ("manager paid staff through the expense account instead of payroll") is itself damaging, even though every word of it is true. Conclusion: when the truthful description of an act is the damaging part, the problem lies in the act, not in how it might be perceived or spun.
- **Role Model Test**: Would the manager teach this method to a new hire on their first day? Argued answer: no one would formalize this into onboarding guidance, and if you would not teach a practice openly, that is itself a sign you know it is wrong.
- **Universality Test**: If every manager did this, Finance could not determine actual labor costs (overtime money would be mixed into general expense lines), no employee could prove income earned this way, and HR would lose the ability to verify whether overtime was distributed fairly across teams. A rule that only functions because almost no one follows it is described as "a loophole," not a legitimate rule.

The debate script also works through a more formal universalizability (Kantian-style) argument in a dedicated section: it argues the opposing side might try to universalize a more sympathetic maxim ("a manager must never let workers absorb the employer's unpaid debt") rather than the manager's actual maxim ("route pay through the expense account without approval"). The rebuttal technique shown: state the actual maxim honestly (what the manager literally did), and show that once stated honestly, universalizing it collapses (no one could tell what labor actually costs, no one could prove their income, fraud and legitimate workarounds would become indistinguishable). This is an example of the classic move in Kantian universalizability critique: an act described too generally can be made to sound acceptable, but the test requires universalizing the actual maxim, not a sanitized version of it.

### 4.7 Step 5 applied: recommended remedy and reflection

The HR side's proposed remedy (used to avoid appearing anti-worker in the debate):
- The team keeps all money already received; nothing is clawed back.
- HR reconstructs the actual overtime hours from available records, reverses the expense-account entry, and processes the payment through payroll at the correct overtime rate (with the company covering any shortfall, since employees should not bear the cost of the manager's chosen method).
- Employees end up with proper payslips reflecting the hours worked.
- The manager receives a written finding that the method was improper, explicitly noting they gained nothing personally and were trying to protect the team; a warning rather than termination, plus training on proper escalation procedures.
- The company addresses the systemic gap: a defined escalation path for urgent/late overtime with a named approver and deadline, an explicit policy that the expense account is never used to pay people, and a review of whether other managers have used the same workaround.

Reflection-oriented takeaway repeated at the close of the debate script: judging an action requires all three dimensions (intent, method, effect), and "one out of three is not an ethical act." A good motive explains why someone acted, it does not make an inaccurate record true.

**The "pocket test" analogy** (used as the debate's closing rhetorical device): take the identical transaction (same expense route, same amount, same description in the books) and change only where the money ultimately went, from the team to the manager's own pocket. Everyone would immediately call that fraud. Since nothing about the recording method, the authorization gap, or the lack of a payroll record changes between the two versions, the argument concludes that the record was already dishonest in the original version too. Where the money went affects how severely the person should be punished; it does not determine whether the record itself was honest.

### 4.8 Argumentation patterns and rebuttal techniques demonstrated

The debate materials also model general argumentation skills relevant to applying ethical frameworks under scrutiny, which may be tested as "how ethical reasoning is defended":

- **Concede-then-remedy opening**: state agreed-upon facts favorable to the opposing side first (the work was real, the manager gained nothing, intentions were good, the company's process may itself be flawed), then immediately state your proposed remedy before the other side can frame you as against the affected employees.
- **Never argue from an unverifiable fact**: since case materials are often incomplete, avoid claiming to know something the source material does not state (for example, whether the manager tried and was refused the normal approval route). If the opposing side is allowed to assume a fact in their favor, you are equally entitled to assume the opposite absent evidence.
- **Distinguish category from formal offense**: e.g., description of conduct as falling into a family of concepts (like "falsification" of records) without alleging a specific legal offense that would require facts not in evidence (like intent or damage requirements).
- **Anticipate the strongest opposing argument and neutralize it early**: e.g., preempting the "HR wanted workers to go unpaid" framing by leading with the full-payment remedy.
- **Cross-examination discipline**: only ask questions answerable from the case materials or from pure logic; never ask a question only the opposing party's hypothetical character (here, "the manager") could truthfully answer, since the opponent could simply invent a favorable answer.

### 4.9 Cautions on overclaiming (from the case brief's "Do not overclaim" section)

The case brief includes a list of claims to avoid because they go beyond what the facts support or misstate applicable rules. These are useful for understanding the difference between a well-reasoned ethical argument and an overreaching one:
- Do not claim to know the literal content of unverified paperwork; stick to what needs no additional facts (overtime pay routed through an expense account, and pay is not an expense).
- Do not claim lost SSS/PhilHealth contributions, since those are generally computed on base pay, not overtime; the safe claim is limited to no payroll record, no tax withheld, no tax certificate entry.
- Do not call the manager a "criminal"; the conduct may fall within a category the law calls falsification (of a private document), but that offense also requires elements like damage or intent to damage, which are arguable, not established, on these facts. Name the category, do not allege the specific offense.
- Do not claim the company automatically loses a tax deduction for failing to withhold tax, since that particular rule was repealed in 2024; a deductible expense still requires records showing what the money was actually for.
- Do not use "wage theft" as if it names a specific legal offense in this context; it is a descriptive term, not a statutory charge.
- Do not overstate statutory entitlement to overtime premium pay: work beyond eight hours is generally paid at the regular wage plus at least twenty-five percent (25%), but managerial staff and field personnel are typically excluded from these statutory overtime rules.

## 5. Applying the Frameworks: Activity 3 Example (AI-Powered Student Surveillance)

A second worked example of applying the Five Ethical Approaches, from a separate class activity, on a different case: whether a university should use AI surveillance during online examinations even though it may invade student privacy and risk incorrectly flagging innocent students.

Question posed: "Should the university use AI surveillance during online examinations even if it may invade students' privacy and incorrectly flag innocent students?"

Application of each approach to this case, as submitted:
- **Utilitarian approach**: AI surveillance protects honest students from unfair grading (i.e., from cheating by others), but a false flag can seriously harm an innocent student. Judged worth it only if errors are rare and are caught before any penalty is applied.
- **Rights approach**: A webcam and microphone active in a student's private bedroom already constitutes a privacy invasion, made worse when there is no clear appeal process for a false flag. Recording under these open-ended terms does not respect a student's autonomy.
- **Fairness/Justice approach**: The system does not affect everyone equally. A disability, a physical tic, or a noisy home environment could cause someone to be flagged simply for existing as they are, not for cheating, which is unfair.
- **Common Good approach**: Fair exams protect the value and credibility of grades for everyone. However, surveillance that students do not trust breeds resentment rather than protecting the common good, so trust must be established first.
- **Virtue approach**: Even an honest student still deserves privacy. A responsible institution does not let an automated system hand down a verdict without human review.
- **Position reached**: Yes, AI surveillance can be used, but only with a human review before any penalty is imposed, deletion of recordings after grading, a real alternative available for students without adequate private space, and clear disclosure of what data is being collected.

This example illustrates the same five-approach evaluation pattern as the overtime case (Section 4.5), applied independently to a different domain (privacy and academic integrity rather than labor and financial records), and shows how a "yes, but with conditions" position can be built from balancing the five approaches rather than picking one as dominant.

## 6. Likely exam angles (cross-cutting synthesis, not from source material verbatim)

The following are structural observations connecting the material above, useful for anticipating exam questions, not additional facts from the sources:

- The five-step framework (recognize, get facts, evaluate alternatives, decide and test, act and reflect) is the backbone that both worked examples (Sections 4 and 5) follow, even though the overtime case works through it far more thoroughly (including a full moot-court style debate) than Activity 3 (which is a short position paper).
- The Five Ethical Approaches (utilitarian, rights, fairness/justice, common good, virtue) are consistently the analytical tool used inside Step 3 of the framework, across both examples. Exam questions may ask you to apply one specific approach to a new scenario, apply all five to compare, or identify which approach a given argument fragment represents.
- The three decision tests (publicity, role model, universality) are distinct from the five approaches. The five approaches are used to evaluate alternative actions during Step 3; the three tests are used in Step 4 to sanity-check the specific decision that was chosen.
- A recurring theme across the lecture and both worked examples is that good intent alone does not make an act ethical: intent, method, and outcome/effect are three separate dimensions, and all three need to hold up for an act to be considered fully ethical (Section 4.3). This "intent is necessary but not sufficient" idea is the thesis of the entire overtime case and is explicitly stated as a discussion point in the official case activity sheet ("Does having a good intention make an action ethical? Why or why not?").
- The overtime case activity explicitly frames itself around the tension between judging an act by its outcome versus judging it by its method (the Challenge Question in Section 2.6/4.5). This is likely to be tested directly, given how central it is to both the activity sheet and the full debate script.
